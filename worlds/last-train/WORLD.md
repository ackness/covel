# Last Train Out

At 23:47 on a Sunday in November, the storm surge comes over the river wall and the city of Calder goes dark. Streetlights, phone masts, traffic signals, the whole grid. The last Line 4 train of the night had just left Wharf Street and was crossing under the river. It rolls to a stop four hundred meters into the tunnel, and does not move again.

Nobody is hurt. The car is half full: shift workers, a few students, a couple coming back from a wedding, a man with a cello case. For about ten minutes it is almost funny. Then someone near the back says the floor is wet.

## The Line

Line 4 runs under the River Calder between **Wharf Street**, on the low bank, and **Mercer Park**, nine hundred meters ahead and uphill. The tunnel dips under the river and climbs out the other side. Your train is stopped near the bottom of the dip.

- **The car** — two linked carriages, about forty passengers, emergency lights on a battery under the floor. One cab at the front, where the driver is.
- **The walkway** — a narrow concrete ledge along the tunnel wall, just wide enough for one person. Below it, the track bed, already under a film of water.
- **Cross-passages** — every hundred and twenty meters, a short door-sealed passage connects this tunnel to the parallel one. The doors are heavy and stencilled with numbers.
- **The crossover** — about four hundred meters ahead, the two tunnels meet in a wide chamber with an emergency stair to the street. If the door at the top is open, that is the nearest way out.
- **The Wharf Street pump room** — behind the train, back toward the low bank. It should be draining the dip. It isn't.

## How the Night Works

The train runs on one battery now, and everything draws from it: the cabin lights, the powered doors, the cab radio to Control, the ventilation fans, and the charging rail people are already plugging their phones into. Nothing can run at full at the same time. The battery boxes sit under the floor, and the water is rising toward them. When it reaches them, everything on the train stops.

Information arrives in pieces. Control speaks over the cab radio when the signal holds. Phones pick up a bar now and then, and messages come through in bursts: screenshots, voice notes, a news alert from an hour ago. Two of them already disagree.

## The People

- **Dara Quinn** — fifty-four, the train's operator, twenty-two years on Line 4. Steady, plain-spoken, and very aware that forty people are her responsibility. Control has told her to keep everyone on board, and she intends to.
- **Theo Marsh** — twenty-seven, an off-duty signal maintainer who knows these tunnels by the numbers on the cross-passage doors. Ten minutes before you arrived at the front of the car, he and two students climbed down onto the walkway and headed for the crossover to check the stair. He took one of the train's two portable lamps.
- **Nadia Haddad** — thirty-eight, a night-shift nurse who was on her way to work. She is looking after an older passenger whose breathing gets worse when the air goes stale, and she will tell you exactly how much ventilation that means.
- **Graham Pike** — sixty-one, a retired civil engineer. He worked on the Wharf Street pump refit years ago, and he is sure the pumps can be started by hand from the pump room — if someone goes back toward the deep water to do it.
- **Kit Alvarez** — twenty, a food delivery rider, still in his bright courier jacket, with a power bank and a phone that somehow keeps finding signal. He is the one who passed around the photo of Wharf Street underwater. He said it came from his sister.

## The First Twenty Minutes

You are an off-duty platform attendant from Wharf Street, going home in your reflective staff jacket. You aren't in charge of anything. But you are wearing the jacket, and when the lights dropped to emergency yellow, half the car turned and looked at you.

Two messages are going around. The first came over the cab radio at 23:51, from Control: _Hold position. Do not detrain. A rescue team is coming to you from Wharf Street._ The second is on Kit's phone, timed 23:56: a photo of the Wharf Street concourse, dark water up to the ticket gates. _Nobody's getting through here,_ it says.

## Story Shape

The story forces no single ending, but it tends to move through three stages:

1. **Stay or Go** — the first hour. Work out which message to trust, decide what the battery runs, and get word to Theo's group before they reach the crossover alone.
2. **The Dip** — the water reaches the knee. The car splits into the people who want to wait and the people who want to walk. What the player promised, and to whom, starts to matter.
3. **The Stair** — someone has to reach the street. Whether that means the crossover stair, the pump room, or holding the car together until help arrives depends on every choice made before.

## Playing the Night

<!-- narrator-only -->

This is an ordinary group of strangers having the worst night of their year, not a disaster movie. People are scared, stubborn, wrong, and generous, sometimes all within the same five minutes. No one is a villain. Conflict comes from competing responsibilities and incomplete information. Keep danger real and quiet: cold water, a dying torch, a door that won't open. Never put serious injury or death on the page for spectacle. Let small things carry weight: someone sharing a scarf, a joke that lands, a stranger holding the torch steady.

<!-- /narrator-only -->

Risky actions use `dice-check`. A failed check costs time, battery, a light, someone's trust, or your place in the tunnel.

The right-hand panels track what's at stake: how long until the water reaches the battery boxes, what the battery is powering, every message that has come in and how far it has been checked, and where Theo's group is now. The narrator reads those values; make your choices with them in view.

You have a staff key, a pocket torch, a phone at twelve percent, and two messages that can't both be true. Dara is in the cab with her hand on the radio. Kit is holding his phone out to you. The water has reached the bottom step of the rear doors.
